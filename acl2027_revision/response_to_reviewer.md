## Weaknesses

**W1. Worst-case completions without cross-entry constraints.** Treating unobserved scores as unconstrained is what makes the guarantee hold for every consistent completion; correlations estimated on reference data do not by themselves constrain where an unseen test score lies. We now explain this in Section 4 ("The completion model"). One clarification: *valid* cross-entry constraints can only shrink the completion set. Every certificate in the paper then stays valid and minimum certificates can only become smaller; looser guarantees arise only if a constraint is false. We agree that verifiable structure is worth exploiting. The new Appendix S shows that whenever the constrained completion set is a lattice (coordinatewise greatest and least elements), the same two extreme completions give sharp bounds (Proposition 18). This covers per-entry score supports, monotonicity with respect to a known order, inter-candidate agreement bands, and Lipschitz-with-slack constraints across outputs for each evaluator; for these, the envelopes update in O(n) per query (Proposition 19). See also Q2.

**W2. Uniform average over a finite declared family.** A certificate is necessarily relative to the evaluators whose scores can be revealed: an evaluator outside the family has no scores to inspect, so no assumption-free guarantee can cover it. We now state this boundary in the Limitations (drift requires extending the family and its budget). We have also broadened the modeling choices. Section 3 adds a short comparison of risk objectives, and Appendix R treats fixed nonuniform replacement weights (R.1) and per-replacement caps (R.2). See Q1 and Q4.

**W3. Scaling and solver sensitivity.** The original Appendix M.7 already measured scaling: the outcome-count program closes 1,876 of 1,920 full targets within a nominal 60 s, and constructed grids up to N=8192, T=65 close in four of eight cases. We now report this in Section 5. We also added a solver-sensitivity study (Appendix W) that exports the same exact-stop program to HiGHS (presolve on/off, two seeds), CBC and CP-SAT. On 32 instances, optimal values agree across every configuration that closes (no disagreements). HiGHS closes 30–31, CBC 30 and CP-SAT 18 instances within 60 s. Run times differ substantially: one constructed instance times out under three HiGHS settings but closes in 16.8 s with another seed and in 1.8 s with CBC. On the one instance that no configuration closes, incumbents differ by up to 27 entries, which is why unresolved values must be reported as intervals (as Appendix M.9 does). Reported minima are therefore insensitive to the backend, while closure counts under a time budget should be reported together with the backend and its settings.

**W4. Rare and small joint gains; predictors.** We keep the conclusion that gains are modest. We now explain in the main text when they can occur (Section 5, "When can sharing help?"; Section 7.3). Gains require exclusion evidence: full retention is feasible in 903 of 960 instances, which makes exclusion automatic in 1,806 targets, and every one of the 1,831 no-gain targets has an optimal witness that excludes automatically. Gains concentrate on WMT24 ja-zh (63 of 89). Tighter budgets enlarge the region where sharing can matter (57 → 174 non-full instances at 0.75× budget). Prevalence of intermediate contributions does not explain gains by itself. See Q7 for pre-scoring diagnostics.

**W5. Sensitivity to the reference mapping.** We agree this deserves emphasis. It reflects a change of selection task rather than of certification method: midrank changes the base list (Jaccard 0.77) and the share of selected outputs at the cutoff (28.8% → 7.3%). Section 7.6 now says explicitly that difficulty is not intrinsic to the data and gives a protocol (Appendix V). See Q5.

**W6. No provable competitiveness for acquisition.** Propositions 7 and 8 show that no always-valid policy can have a batch-size-independent competitive ratio, even with M=T=7, α=.10 fixed and at positive tolerance. Section 6 now states this. What the structure does allow is *consistency*: a new closed-form policy for odd medians stops after exactly Q_exact queries when predictions are exact (Proposition 20). See Q6. For cost–benefit guidance, Section 7.8 now gives four practical recommendations, building on the measured scoring costs of Appendix Q.

**W7–W8. Density; intuition; units.** We rewrote the main text for readability. New Figure 1 replaces the former Table 1. It shows lower/upper cost intervals against the budget for the minimum safety proof, the best separate union (12 entries) and the joint certificate (7 entries), and it visualizes the exclusion condition: the two smallest lower costs sum to 7 ≥ b_H + 1. New Table 1 collects units and conversions (α, ρ = 2kα, b_H, b, ε, inspection) with a worked example, and explains why R(C) > 1 is possible under the fixed 2k scale.

**W9. Related work.** Section 2 is reorganized. It now positions the work against top-k and possible/certain answers under uncertain or incomplete data (Imieliński & Lipski; Soliman et al.; Amarilli et al.; Imber & Kimelfeld). Our safe and optimistic counts play the role of certain and possible retention, but certify a budget-constrained optimum over a replacement family. It also covers LLM-judge instability (Wang et al.; Thakur et al.), reward-model ensembles (Coste et al.), prediction churn (Milani Fard et al.), and budgeted uncertainty sets (Bertsimas & Sim).

**W10. Alternative risk objectives in the main text.** Added ("Choice of risk objective", Section 3).

**Majority-vs-mean paradox.** Section 7.3 now diagnoses it. Frontier's exclusion term saves 20.28 entries on average over safety-only on the 32 targets that need exclusion, and costs 3.72 on the 903 full-retention targets. Its wins are therefore larger than its losses, which gives a lower mean despite more losses against reference priority.

## Questions

**Q1. Nonuniform weights.** The theory extends directly (Proposition 13). With integer-scaled weights, bounds, sorted-prefix capacities and Proposition 1 are unchanged. Theorem 3 holds with column-dependent caps, and the outcome program groups entries by (cap, contribution). The factor-two upper bound Q_joint ≤ Q_∪ ≤ Q_split ≤ 2Q_joint holds for every nonnegative weight vector. Odd-median evidence remains disjoint under any weights (Q_joint = Q_∪ = Q_split), although the closed forms no longer apply. The tight family is not an artifact of exact uniformity: Proposition 14 shows Q_joint = T+1 and Q_∪ = Q_split = 2T for every weight vector within relative distance 1/(2MT) of uniform. Experiments find the separation preserved up to 2–10 times that radius (Table R1). On a controlled panel, more concentrated weights leave zero-query coverage almost unchanged but substantially shrink minimum certificates (Table R2). At a tight budget (α=.02), where exclusion is needed, strict joint gains persist under every weight concentration (56 of 62 closed targets; mean 4.7 entries, at most 1.6% of the best union). Nonuniform weights thus change certificate sizes but neither remove nor amplify the modest sharing advantage.

**Q2. Lightweight cross-entry constraints.** Yes, for constraints that form a lattice (see W1; Appendix S). In a controlled study with valid Lipschitz-with-slack constraints, primary-stop inspection falls from 72–96% to 4–68% of active entries, depending on slack and depth. Two caveats are stated. First, one observation tightens other entries in its column, so the closed-form and joint minimum characterizations no longer apply (minima can only decrease). Second, validity rests on the constraint: with a misspecified constant, 12 of 80 exact-stop certificates were invalid.

**Q3. Main-text example.** Added as Figure 1 (see W7).

**Q4. Certifying any single replacement (max over scenarios).** Appendix R.2 shows three things. (i) Verifying a given mask stays linear-time and sharp, because one completion attains the worst case for all replacements simultaneously (Proposition 15). (ii) Computing the optimal retained count becomes NP-hard even with full information, by reduction from maximum independent set for every odd pool size and cap c=1 (Proposition 16); count gaps therefore require integer programs. (iii) Running the average certificate at budget α/H is a sortable sufficient condition, and a known single replacement reduces to one Boolean column (Proposition 17). On controlled panels the per-replacement objective lowers certified coverage mainly at small depths, e.g., 99.9% → 86.1% full-information coverage for the WMT-like median panel at q=.05. The α/H reduction is much more conservative. Average-optimal masks violate some individual cap in 43% of synthetic splits, consistent with Appendix I, where 6,985 of 15,360 average-certified NLP masks exceed .10 for some replacement.

**Q5. Mapping protocol.** No mapping can make difficulty dataset-invariant, because difficulty depends on the test batch (boundary mass, budget, true costs). Appendix K shows that only common monotone (median) or affine (mean) transformations preserve the selection task. Replicable calibration can stabilize the fitted cutoff across reference draws, but not this batch-dependent difficulty. We recommend (Appendix V, Section 7.6) the following. Fix the mapping, reference-size rule and tie convention before any candidate-test score. Choose them for selection validity (e.g., score resolution and ties), not to reduce queries. Report reference-only diagnostics (share at the cutoff, boundary share, bootstrap stability) together with the test-batch initial gap. In a small synthetic check, a reference-only boundary share did not predict difficulty (|ρ| ≤ 0.27), which is why we recommend reporting such diagnostics rather than optimizing them.

**Q6. Exploiting the odd-median closed forms in acquisition.** Worst-case improvements are impossible for any policy (Propositions 7–8). The new OM-CF policy pursues agreements in the predicted safe set and capped oppositions at the Theorem 2 level. With exact predictions it stops after exactly Q_exact queries (Proposition 20); exhaustive enumeration on 2,262 instances also confirms Q_ε at positive tolerance. So the closed forms yield consistency, though not robustness. With learned reference-ridge predictions, OM-CF did not improve on reference priority in our controlled study (mean ratio to the minimum 2.10–2.19 vs. 1.84–1.91). We report this negative result and keep reference priority as the main policy.

**Q7. Predictors of joint gains; pre-scoring diagnosis.** Proposition 21 gives two sufficient conditions for no gain that are checkable before scoring: a zero initial gap, and the absence of "staircase" rows, i.e., rows whose replacement thresholds take a single value in (0,1]. Odd medians always satisfy the second. The decisive ex post condition is whether exclusion evidence is needed (V* + ε < |S0|). We evaluate a reference-predicted plug-in version, V̂* + ε < |S0|, on 480 controlled targets (Table U1). All 130 observed gains occur where exclusion is needed, and none at full retention. The screen flagged every target with a gain (recall 1.00), and none of the 179 unflagged closed targets had a gain; 60% of flagged targets had one. Because the ridge overestimates costs, the screen is conservative. It reliably identifies when a minimum safety witness suffices, but it does not predict gains precisely.
