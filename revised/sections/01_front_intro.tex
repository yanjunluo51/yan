\title{A curvature-based real-options method for member-specific thresholds and contested-price intervals in stage-gate screening under regulatory ambiguity}

\begin{abstract}
Stage-gate committees screen irreversible investments before regulatory conditions settle. A representative trigger or aggregate score may hide whether members classify the prevailing price alike. This study constructs member-specific triggers as affine combinations of the two endpoint triggers of an ordered volatility ambiguity set. A perpetuity mapping converts them into price thresholds, whose extremes bound a contested-price interval. At a positive radius, a unique critical weight places a member trigger at the no-ambiguity anchor, and trigger curvature determines its location. Under the affine rule, the analysis placed this weight above one half in standard-deviation units and below it in variance units. A pre-commitment $\alpha$-maxmin expected utility ($\aMEU$) benchmark placed it below one half in both coordinates, at a reference value equal to investment cost. Against this benchmark, the largest relative trigger error across all calibrations on the primary radius range was 2.74\%. Small trigger errors did not guarantee identical classifications. Two prespecified classification-fidelity criteria held at the evaluated radii up to 10\%. A finite registry routes wide-radius, near-threshold, and unregistered gates to benchmark recomputation. The member-threshold vector reduced the median simulated price-axis loss by 88.5\% against the ex post best of four single-trigger summaries. In a carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) retrofit, every archived price stayed below the lower interval edge in the reference case. In 15 of 32 sensitivity configurations, the highest archived price reached or exceeded this edge. The report thus shows member disagreement at the prevailing price before any vote and states its release conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large, irreversible investments are commonly reviewed through a sequence of stage gates \citep{Cooper2008}. At each gate, a committee decides whether to commit to the project now or to defer it to a later review. When the decision concerns timing, real-options analysis reduces it to a single investment trigger \citep{McDonaldSiegel1986,DixitPindyck1994}. Commitment is warranted once project value reaches this trigger. A single trigger gives the review one compact criterion, which can be documented and recomputed at successive gates. When project cash flows are linked to an observable price, the screening boundary can be restated on the price axis. The committee then reads it in units it already monitors. In this paper, a trigger is a project-value quantity, and a threshold is its image on the price axis.

Committee members may receive the same engineering, cost, and policy evidence and still place different weights on adverse regulatory scenarios. A single trigger can conceal these differences. A conventional real-options analysis returns one project-level trigger, usually under a representative ambiguity attitude. Member-level dispersion therefore disappears at the modeling stage. Committee decision-support procedures may retain individual assessments but aggregate them into a score, a ranking, or a vote before reporting. In both cases, the assessments are compressed before anyone checks whether their implied classifications agree.

Member-level dispersion need not change the classification at a given gate. Distinct member thresholds can still lie on the same side of the prevailing price. A price below every member threshold places all members in the wait region. A price at or above every threshold places all members in the invest region. A mixed classification arises only between the lowest and the highest member thresholds. Whether one classification suffices therefore depends on the position of the price relative to the whole threshold set. An aggregate score replaces this set with one number and loses the thresholds. The set remains recoverable, however, from the recorded calibration, ambiguity radius, and member weights. It is conditional on these recorded inputs, and changing them can change the classification of the same prevailing price.

This paper examined when member heterogeneity changes the current gate classification. It also examined how this information can be reported without aggregating member thresholds in advance. Both questions are posed with an ordered ambiguity set common to all members. Members differ only in their recorded weights on the adverse endpoint of this set. Member triggers follow a closed-form affine endpoint rule. Its accuracy is measured against the $\alpha$-maxmin expected utility ($\aMEU$) optimum under the same model inputs. The two rules can classify the same price differently. We therefore also determined the conditions under which the affine report may be released alone, and the computation required at other gates.

The model represents regulatory uncertainty as an ordered interval of forward volatilities around an anchor. One component of regulatory uncertainty thus enters through the volatility channel. The committee records one weight for each member. This weight applies to the adverse endpoint of the interval, where the option to wait has the lowest value. Lower volatility reduces the value of waiting and lowers the trigger. The adverse endpoint is therefore the low-volatility endpoint in this timing model.

The running example is a committee screening a carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) retrofit of a coal-fired power plant. The retrofit costs $1.585\times10^{9}$\,CNY and captures $1.69\times10^{6}$ tonnes of CO$_2$ per year \citep{Sun2024}. Project cash flows track an observable carbon price. The member triggers therefore become marginal carbon-price thresholds in Chinese Yuan per tonne (CNY/t). The smallest and largest thresholds bound the contested-price interval.

The same construction identifies whether each member trigger lies above or below the no-ambiguity anchor. The trigger is strictly increasing in volatility, so the two endpoint triggers differ. Exactly one weight in $[0,1]$, the critical weight, places a member trigger at the anchor. This weight splits the members into two groups around the anchor, and the split carries over to the price thresholds. One half is the weight at which the two endpoint triggers count equally.

In the application, the ambiguity radii of the gates come from a policy-text series. Its document identifiers and dates are recorded, so a reader with database access can retrieve the same documents.

The application produced two main results. The first concerned unanimity. Under the reference configuration, every archived price stayed below the contemporaneous lower interval edge. Unanimity was still configuration-dependent. In 15 of the 32 sensitivity configurations, the highest archived price reached or exceeded the lower edge. The second result concerned the common inputs, which moved the interval edges further than member disagreement did. A 10\% reduction in the cost-to-volume ratio $I/Q$ moved the lower edge by 11.34\,CNY/t. The widest interval produced by the reference profile along the reconstructed path was 6.63\,CNY/t.

The paper makes two contributions. The first is an analytical characterization of the critical weight. Its uniqueness follows from the strict monotonicity of the affine rule, and trigger curvature locates it relative to one half. This curvature changes sign between the standard-deviation and variance coordinates. The direction of each member's shift is therefore a property of the declared coordinate. The second contribution is a member-indexed reporting design. The member-threshold vector and the contested-price interval bounded by its extremes show whether the prevailing price receives the same classification under every member assessment. Benchmark comparisons and a classification-fidelity analysis quantify the accuracy of this report in price units. In this analysis, small trigger errors could still change the invest-or-wait outcome. A finite-registry safeguard converts these limits into a release rule. Conditioning release on registered configurations may make each release auditable after the fact.

The study therefore treats disagreement among committee members as a reported quantity with stated accuracy limits. Existing work on real options and on committee decision support mostly reports project-level objects. The reporting gap addressed here lies between these two literatures.
