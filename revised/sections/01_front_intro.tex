\title{A curvature-based real-options method for member-specific thresholds and contested-price intervals in stage-gate screening under regulatory ambiguity}

\begin{abstract}
Stage-gate committees screen irreversible investments before regulatory conditions settle. A representative trigger or an aggregate score may hide whether members classify the prevailing price alike. Recovering this information requires one threshold per member. This study constructs member-specific triggers as affine combinations of the two endpoint triggers of an ordered volatility ambiguity set. A perpetuity mapping converts them into price thresholds, and the extreme thresholds bound a contested-price interval. At a positive ambiguity radius, a unique critical weight places a member trigger at the no-ambiguity anchor, and trigger curvature determines its location. The analysis placed this weight above one half in standard-deviation units and below one half in variance units. A pre-commitment $\alpha$-maxmin expected utility ($\aMEU$) benchmark, evaluated at a reference value equal to the investment cost, placed it below one half in both coordinates. Against this benchmark, the largest relative trigger error on the primary evaluation grid was 2.74\%. Small trigger errors still did not guarantee identical classifications. Two prespecified classification-fidelity criteria held up to a 10\% relative ambiguity radius. A finite registry routes wide-radius, near-threshold, and unregistered gates to benchmark recomputation. In simulated panels, the member-threshold vector reduced the median price-axis loss by 88.5\% relative to the ex post best of four single-trigger summaries. In a carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) retrofit, every archived carbon price stayed below the lower interval edge under the reference configuration. In 15 of 32 sensitivity configurations, the highest archived price reached or exceeded this edge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large, irreversible investments are commonly reviewed through a sequence of stage gates \citep{Cooper2008}. At each gate, a committee decides whether to commit to the project now or to defer it to a later review. When the decision concerns timing, real-options analysis reduces it to a single investment trigger \citep{McDonaldSiegel1986,DixitPindyck1994}. Commitment is warranted once project value reaches this trigger. A single trigger gives the review one compact criterion, which can be documented and recomputed at successive gates. When project cash flows are linked to an observable price, the screening boundary can be restated on the price axis. The committee then reads it in units it already monitors. In this paper, a trigger is a project-value quantity, and a threshold is its image on the price axis.

Committee members often receive the same engineering, cost, and policy evidence. They may still place different weights on adverse regulatory scenarios, and a single trigger can conceal these differences. A conventional real-options analysis returns one project-level trigger, usually under a representative ambiguity attitude. Member-level dispersion therefore disappears at the modeling stage. Committee decision-support procedures may retain individual assessments. They typically aggregate these assessments into a score, a ranking, or a vote before reporting. In both cases, the assessments are compressed before anyone checks whether their implied classifications agree. The resulting summary cannot show whether aggregation preserves the member-level screening outcomes.

Member-level dispersion need not change the classification at a given gate. Distinct member thresholds can still lie on the same side of the prevailing price. A price below every member threshold places all members in the wait region. A price at or above every threshold places all members in the invest region. A mixed classification arises only between the lowest and the highest member thresholds. Whether one classification suffices therefore depends on the position of the price relative to the whole threshold set. An aggregate score replaces this set with one number and loses the thresholds. The set can be rebuilt from the recorded calibration, ambiguity radius, and member weights. It is conditional on these recorded inputs, and changing them can change the classification of the same prevailing price.

This paper examined when member heterogeneity changes the current gate classification. It also examined how this information can be reported without aggregating member thresholds in advance. Both questions are posed with an ordered ambiguity set common to all members. Members differ only in their recorded weights on the adverse endpoint of this set. Member triggers follow a closed-form affine rule, which approximates a separately optimized benchmark. The two rules can therefore classify the same price differently. We also determined the conditions under which the affine report may be released alone, and the computation required at a gate outside these conditions.

The model represents regulatory uncertainty as an ordered interval of forward volatilities around an anchor. One component of regulatory uncertainty thus enters through the volatility channel. The committee records one weight for each member. This weight applies to the adverse endpoint of the interval, where the option to wait has the lowest value. Lower volatility reduces the value of waiting and lowers the trigger. The adverse endpoint is therefore the low-volatility endpoint in this timing model.

An affine endpoint rule maps each recorded weight to a member trigger. The rule is stipulated as a reporting convention. Its accuracy is measured against the $\alpha$-maxmin expected utility ($\aMEU$) optimum under the same model inputs.

The running example is a committee screening a carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) retrofit of a coal-fired power plant. The retrofit costs $1.585\times10^{9}$\,CNY and captures $1.69\times10^{6}$ tonnes of CO$_2$ per year \citep{Sun2024}. Project cash flows track an observable carbon price. The member triggers therefore become marginal carbon-price thresholds in Chinese Yuan per tonne (CNY/t). The smallest and largest thresholds bound the contested-price interval. Outside this interval, one classification suffices at the current gate. The thresholds still remain distinct, and the price may fall between them at a later gate.

The same construction identifies whether each member trigger lies above or below the no-ambiguity anchor. The trigger is strictly increasing in volatility, so the two endpoint triggers differ. Exactly one weight in $[0,1]$, the critical weight, places a member trigger at the anchor. This partition carries over to the price thresholds. One half is the weight at which the two endpoint triggers count equally. Strict convexity determines the side of one half on which the critical weight falls. This side depends on the coordinate in which the committee declares its ambiguity set.

In the application, the ambiguity radii of the gates come from a policy-text series. Its document identifiers and dates are recorded, so a reader with database access can retrieve the same documents. This series is a scenario input specific to the application. Any other nonnegative series satisfying the same admissibility condition could replace it.

The application produced two findings. The first concerned unanimity. Under the reference configuration, every archived price stayed below the contemporaneous lower interval edge. Unanimity was still configuration-dependent. In 15 of the 32 sensitivity configurations, the highest archived price reached or exceeded the lower edge. The second finding concerned the common inputs, which moved the interval edges further than member disagreement did. A 10\% reduction in the cost-to-volume ratio $I/Q$ moved the lower edge by 11.34\,CNY/t. The widest interval produced by the reference profile along the reconstructed path was 6.63\,CNY/t (\SM{S6.4}).

The paper makes two contributions. The first is an analytical result on the critical weight. Strict monotonicity of the affine rule makes the critical weight unique. Trigger curvature in volatility determines its position relative to one half, and this curvature changes sign between the standard-deviation and variance coordinates. The direction of each member's shift relative to the anchor is therefore a property of the declared coordinate. The second contribution is a member-indexed reporting design. The member-threshold vector and the contested-price interval bounded by its extremes show whether the prevailing price receives the same classification under every member assessment. A pre-commitment $\aMEU$ benchmark, solved at each recorded weight, provides the accuracy reference for the stipulated affine rule. A classification-fidelity analysis restates this accuracy in the units a gate reports. In this analysis, small trigger errors could still change the invest-or-wait outcome. A finite-registry safeguard converts these limits into a release rule. Conditioning release on registered configurations makes each decision auditable.
